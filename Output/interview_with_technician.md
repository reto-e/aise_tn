# Stakeholder Interview Transcript — Technician

**Interviewee:** Marco Ferretti, Technician  
**Interviewer:** Requirements Engineer  
**Topic:** Daily workflow and pain points with the Torqvoice workshop management system  
**Format:** Text-based simulation (AI-played stakeholder)

---

**Q: What do you do first when you come to the workshop?**

First thing? I check the board. The physical one — the whiteboard by the entrance. Keys on hooks, registration plates written up, names next to jobs. That tells me what's coming in today.

I don't open the system straight away, if I'm honest. By the time I've logged in and found the right screen, Davide — the advisor — has already told me verbally what the first car needs. It's faster that way.

I'll open Torqvoice maybe ten, fifteen minutes later, once the car's on the lift, to check if there's any history. You know — was it here before? Any old findings I should know about? That part's actually useful.

But the morning briefing? That's still the whiteboard. Always has been.

---

**Q: How does this information get on the whiteboard?**

Davide puts it up. Every evening before he leaves, or first thing in the morning when he comes in. He looks at whatever's been booked — in the system, phone calls, walk-ins — and writes it up by hand.

Sometimes it matches what's in Torqvoice, sometimes it doesn't. If a customer called last minute, Davide writes it on the board but maybe hasn't had time to create the job in the system yet. So I go to the screen, the car's not there. I have to wait for him to add it, or I just start from what he told me and fill in the record later.

And if Davide is sick? Then whoever covers him doesn't always know the routine. Last month we had a car come in that nobody had written up anywhere — not the board, not the system. Customer just showed up. We sorted it out, but it cost us half an hour.

There's no connection between what the customer books and what I see in the morning. That's just how it is here.

---

**Q: How much lead time do you need to prepare your day carefully?**

Honestly? The day before is ideal. If I know by five o'clock what's coming in tomorrow, I can check the parts, make sure everything's on the shelf, maybe pull the vehicle history in advance. That way when the car arrives I'm not scrambling.

Same-day I can work with, if I know by seven, seven-thirty at the latest. Gives me time to at least check if I have the parts before the customer is already standing there.

What I can't work with is finding out when the car rolls in. That happens more than it should. Customer books online — apparently we have some kind of booking thing now — and I find out when I hear the tyres on the forecourt. That's no good. If it's a brake job and I don't have the pads in stock, the car sits here all day and the customer is angry at me, not at the booking system.

The parts thing is the real issue. Labour I can shuffle around. But if I'm missing a part, the whole job stops. So the earlier I know, the better. Previous day would change my life, honestly. But I'd settle for first thing in the morning.

---

**Q: So what do you do if you do not have the parts in stock?**

I stop the job. Lift the car back down, move it to the side, go find Davide. Tell him we're missing — say, a brake calliper, or a specific filter. He calls the supplier, checks if they can do a same-day delivery. Sometimes yes, sometimes we're waiting until tomorrow.

Meanwhile the customer has dropped off their car, maybe left to work or whatever, expecting to pick it up at five. Now we have to call them and explain. That conversation is never fun.

The thing is, I check Torqvoice for parts sometimes — there's an inventory section. But it's not always up to date. If someone used the last two filters last week and didn't record it properly, the system says we have stock when we don't. So I've learned not to fully trust it. I still go physically check the shelf before I start a job. Which defeats the purpose a bit, doesn't it.

And ordering — I can't do that myself in the system. I have to tell Davide or the parts manager, they order it. There's no way for me to raise a request directly and have it tracked. It's all verbal, all WhatsApp messages, bits of paper. Half the time I don't know if the order was placed or not until the delivery shows up — or doesn't.

---

**Q: How good are customers in estimating the time it takes to inspect or fix their car?**

Not good. Not good at all.

Most of them have no idea. They say "it's just a quick check" and then I open the bonnet and find three things that need attention. Or they say "it's a major job" and actually it's forty-five minutes.

The worst is when they book a specific time slot online — I heard we're doing that now — and they write in the notes "should only take an hour." And they've booked themselves a collection at lunchtime. But it's an inspection plus whatever I find. I can't know how long it takes until I've done it.

Inspections are the worst for this. A basic inspection I can do in an hour if everything is fine. But if I find something — worn bushings, a leaky seal, whatever — and the customer says yes, fix it now, that's suddenly a half-day job. Where do I put the other cars?

We had a customer last month, booked two hours for a "general check." I ended up needing six. She was calling every hour asking if the car was ready. Davide had to manage her the whole afternoon.

What would help me is if the system gave customers realistic time estimates when they book. Not what the customer thinks — what we think. Based on the service type. That way at least expectations are set before they walk in.

---

**Q: How do you think could the system give them a realistic time estimate?**

I mean... we already know roughly how long things take. An oil change is forty-five minutes. A full inspection is an hour, maybe ninety minutes. A clutch replacement is a day. That's just experience — every mechanic knows this.

So why not put those numbers in the system? When the workshop sets up a service type — "brake inspection," "timing belt," whatever — they enter a standard duration. One hour, three hours, half day. Then when the customer picks that service to book, the system shows them: "This service typically takes two hours." Simple.

And maybe add a buffer. Because customers never tell you everything upfront. They book an oil change and then mention at drop-off — oh, there's also a noise from the front right, can you check that? So if the system says two hours, I'd want two and a half in my schedule. Otherwise I'm already behind by ten o'clock.

The other thing is — and I don't know if the system can do this — some jobs depend on the car. A timing belt on a Golf is three hours. On some Volvos it's a full day. So maybe the estimate should depend on the make and model too, not just the service type.

But I'm a mechanic, not a programmer. I just know what I need on the floor. You tell me if that's possible.
